Tech ethics

Ethics helps people decide what they ought to do, including when a choice is legal but may still cause harm.

Questions to ask

  • Fairness Could this system treat people differently or exclude a group?
  • Privacy Is personal information used in ways people understand and expect?
  • Safety What could go wrong, who could be harmed, and how can that risk be reduced?
  • Accountability Who is responsible for monitoring the system and correcting problems?
A responsible decision considers affected people, likely consequences, transparency, and ways to prevent or address harm.

Case file: The résumé filter

A company uses software to rank job applications. The team notices that qualified applicants from one school are consistently ranked lower, although the school is not part of the stated job requirements.

Socratic check: What evidence would tell you whether the ranking is fair? Who should review the results? Should the team pause use of the tool while investigating?

Worked example: Is all-day location tracking justified?

Situation: A campus navigation app records a student's precise location all day, including when the app is closed. Location history may reveal sensitive routines, and continuous tracking is not needed to give directions on request.

Ethical issue — what should the team do? Consider privacy, user autonomy, transparency, and avoidable harm. Would students reasonably expect background tracking? Could the team provide directions while collecting less data?

Relevant law — what rule may apply? Where the GDPR applies, Article 5(1)(c) says personal data must be “adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary” for its purposes (the data-minimisation principle). Whether the GDPR applies, and whether processing is lawful, depends on the circumstances; other requirements, including a lawful basis, may also matter.

The distinction: Ethics asks what is fair and respectful, including beyond the legal minimum. Law sets enforceable requirements in its jurisdiction. A practice can raise ethical concerns even if it is lawful; an ethically motivated design does not by itself prove legal compliance.

Possible improvement: Request location only while the person is using turn-by-turn directions, explain why it is needed, and avoid retaining a location history unless it is genuinely necessary and properly justified.

Source: GDPR, Article 5(1)(c), official regulation text. See the Citations tab for the full reference.

Technology and law

Digital activities can involve many areas of law. The rules that apply depend on the facts and the jurisdiction.

Common areas to consider

  • Privacy Rules may limit how personal data is collected, protected, shared, or retained.
  • Copyright Creative works and software may be protected; permission or a license may be needed to reuse them.
  • Computer access Accessing systems or data without authorization can have serious consequences.
  • Consumer protection Claims about a product, service, or automated decision may be subject to legal requirements.
For a real legal question, consult current official guidance or a qualified legal professional in the relevant jurisdiction.

Case file: Two kinds of information

Privacy example: A fitness app collects precise location data even though its basic step counter does not need it. The team should examine what is collected, why it is needed, and how users are informed. The GDPR is one legal framework that addresses personal-data processing; its application depends on the circumstances.

Trade-secret example: A small manufacturer keeps a valuable production formula in a shared folder that every intern and contractor can access. Before relying on trade-secret protections, the business should consider whether it is taking reasonable steps to keep the information secret. The DTSA is a U.S. law relevant to trade secrets; actual rights depend on the facts and applicable law.

What changed and why

This page records how peer feedback informed the latest improvement to the academy.

Feedback received

A classmate who reviewed the website said it lacked real-world examples for each section.

How the feedback was addressed

  • Added short, recognizable case files about a campus location app, a résumé-ranking tool, personal data, and a confidential manufacturing formula.
  • Added a bonus product-launch scenario to connect privacy, safety, and accountability.
  • Added Socratic questions so learners practice identifying affected people, testing assumptions, asking for evidence, and proposing safeguards.
  • Connected legal examples to the GDPR and DTSA citations while noting that laws depend on jurisdiction and facts.
  • Added a worked example that explains an ethical privacy issue and GDPR Article 5(1)(c)'s data-minimisation principle, then distinguishes ethical judgment from legal compliance.
  • Linked the GDPR claim directly to the official regulation text and qualified the explanation to avoid implying that the GDPR applies in every situation or that one principle alone determines compliance.
  • Expanded practice into scenario-based decisions with answer-specific explanations and reflection questions, including a location-tracking example that applies the privacy discussion.

Learning approach

The examples are realistic teaching scenarios, not claims about specific companies, court cases, or legal outcomes. Each practice choice now receives an explanation and a reflection question, so learners can consider why an option is stronger or riskier and apply the reasoning to a new situation.
